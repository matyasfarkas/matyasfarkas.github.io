    A 45-minute walkthrough of the Global Macroeconomic Analysis Projections and Scenarios (GMAPS) tool¶

    A minute-by-minute presenter's script for a first-time audience.

    Who this is for. People who forecast or think about the economy — desk economists, policy analysts, students — who have never opened the tool before. No statistics or programming background is assumed. Everything is explained in plain words.

    The one idea to keep in the back of your mind. The tool starts from a careful statistical forecast of the economy, and then lets you ask "what if?" — what if growth is stronger, what if the central bank cuts rates, what if we believe an outside forecast — and it shows you a consistent answer: not just the number you changed, but everything that moves with it, at home and abroad.

    How to run the session. Present live, with the tool on screen. Every block below tells you three things, in Jesper Lindé's spirit: what you do, how you do it, and what you find. Times are cumulative (so "12–16" means minutes twelve to sixteen). Keep your own cursor moving — this tool is meant to be touched, not admired.

    Presenter setup (before the audience arrives). Open the tool in a wide browser window. Make sure the country selector says United States. Click Reset all once so you start from a clean baseline. Have this script on a second screen.

    Part 1 — What am I looking at? (0–8 min)¶

    0–3 min · The big picture, in one breath¶

    What you do. Set the scene before you touch anything.

    Say this (plain language):

    "Every central bank and finance ministry publishes a forecast — growth next year, inflation, interest rates. But a forecast is really a starting point. The interesting questions come after: what if it turns out differently? What if oil spikes, what if the Fed holds rates higher for longer, what if China slows? This tool answers those questions.

    Under the hood there is a statistical model that has read decades of data for fifty economies and learned how they normally move together — when growth rises, what usually happens to inflation a year later; when the US sneezes, who catches a cold. We don't need to know the equations. We just need to know that the model captures the normal relationships, and the tool lets us lean on them."

    What you find. You've told them the tool is a scenario machine built on a learned-from-data forecast. That's the whole mental model. Everything else is detail.

    3–8 min · Orient the screen¶

    What you do. Give a slow tour of the three regions of the screen. Don't click yet — just point.

    How. Move your cursor as you name each part:

    • The top bar (dark blue). On the left, the Country selector — right now, United States. On the right, three small choices: Units (how the numbers are expressed), Uncertainty (whether to draw confidence bands), and a Compute button. Also up here: Data coverage, the User Manual, and Docs.
    • The left panel (white). This is your control room. From top to bottom: your Scenario (name, save, load, reset), Long-run targets, External forecasts, Cross-border spillovers, and Policy counterfactual. We'll visit each.
    • The middle (the charts). One small chart per economic variable — Inflation, Policy Rate, GDP Growth, and more, scrolling down. Each chart shows the past (a little grey history on the left) and the future (the forecast on the right).

    Say this about a single chart (point at GDP Growth):

    "Read one chart and you can read them all. The grey dotted line is the model's own forecast — its best guess if nothing special happens. The shaded grey band, when we turn it on, is its uncertainty — 'we're fairly sure the truth is in here.' In a moment we'll add a coloured line — that's your scenario, once you start asking 'what if?'"

    What you find. The audience can now look at any chart and know: grey = the model's baseline, colour = my scenario, band = uncertainty, left = history, right = forecast. That literacy carries the whole session.

    Part 2 — Your first scenario: nudge the economy (8–16 min)¶

    8–12 min · Drag a dot, watch the model respond¶

    What you do. Make GDP growth stronger next year and let the model tell you what else changes.

    How. 1. Find the GDP Growth chart. 2. Turn conditioning on for this chart — the quickest way is to right-click on the forecast (right-hand) part of the chart. That does two things at once: it switches this chart into "I'm steering this" mode, and it drops a point exactly where you clicked. (The other way is to click the small condition button on the chart first.) Once conditioning is on, a small "drag to set path" tag appears. 3. Now click and drag that forecast dot upward — say, to around 3%. Let go.

    Presenter note (worth knowing): a chart is "read-only" until you turn conditioning on — you can't drag a fresh chart cold. Right-click is the one-move shortcut: it activates and places the point in a single click. If a chart won't let you drag, it just means conditioning isn't on yet.

    Say this while you drag:

    "I'm not editing a spreadsheet cell. I'm telling the model, 'suppose growth is stronger here.' The model now does something a spreadsheet can't: it works out what else is consistent with that. Watch the other charts."

    What you find. - The GDP Growth line now runs through your new point — a coloured scenario line has appeared. - Look at Inflation and the Policy Rate: they moved too, on their own. Stronger growth usually brings a bit more inflation, and a central bank that leans against it — so rates drift up. You changed one number; the model moved the whole picture, the way history says it should.

    Say this (the punchline):

    "That's the difference between a guess and a model-consistent scenario. I don't have to remember that faster growth feeds inflation with a lag — the model already learned it from the data. I just express the idea, and it keeps the story coherent."

    12–16 min · Shape the path, and read it back¶

    What you do. Turn a one-quarter nudge into a story and inspect it.

    How. - Drag a second dot, a few quarters later, to hold growth up for a while (a sustained boom, not a one-off blip). - Hover over any dot — a little label shows the exact value. - On the left panel, scroll to the Conditioning section: every quarter you pinned is listed with a slider, so you can fine-tune by number instead of by mouse. - To undo, use Reset at the top of the left panel (clears this scenario; keeps everything else).

    Say this:

    "Dragging is for sketching; the sliders on the left are for precision. And notice the chart title turns amber on anything you've touched — that's the tool being honest about which lines are your judgement versus the model's own read."

    What you find. You now have a hand-shaped growth path, and every related variable has followed it into a consistent scenario. The audience has made the model talk.

    Part 3 — Where does it all settle? Long-run targets (16–23 min)¶

    16–20 min · Tell the model where things end up¶

    What you do. Pin down the far future — the level inflation or rates return to — without touching the near-term path.

    How. 1. On the left panel, open Long-run targets. 2. Find Inflation. Each control shows the model's own long-run estimate next to it (e.g. "est 2.4"). Type a number, or drag the little slider, to set where inflation settles — say, 2.0% (a central bank's target).

    Say this:

    "The near-term forecast is about momentum — what's happening right now. The long run is about anchors — where the economy comes to rest once the dust settles. Sometimes the model's own resting point drifts a little from what we believe policy will deliver. Here I can say, gently, 'in the long run, inflation comes home to 2%,' and the tool tilts the whole path toward that without breaking the near-term story."

    What you find. Every chart for that variable shifts smoothly so that the tail of the forecast lands on your target, while the first few quarters barely move. It's a lean, not a yank.

    20–23 min · The shortcut: consensus anchors¶

    What you do. Apply a ready-made, credible set of long-run values in one click.

    How. If the panel shows "Apply consensus anchors," click it. It fills in publicly known long-run values — the inflation target, trend growth, the neutral interest rate, normal unemployment — all at once. A small badge confirms they're applied; Clear reverts to the model's own estimates.

    Say this:

    "You don't have to invent these numbers. 'Consensus anchors' loads the values everyone already agrees on for the long run, so your scenario starts from a sensible, defensible resting place."

    What you find. The forecasts settle onto familiar, sensible long-run levels. This is the quiet feature that keeps scenarios from wandering off into implausible territory.

    Part 4 — Bring in an outside forecast (23–31 min)¶

    This is the newest capability, and the one most people will use every round. Slow down here.

    23–26 min · The idea: a forecast as an ingredient¶

    What you do. Frame the problem before the demo.

    Say this:

    "You almost never start from a blank page. The IMF has just published its World Economic Outlook; your own desk has a number; the market has a view. The old way was to throw those away and re-draw everything by hand. Here, you can hand the model someone else's forecast and say: 'take this seriously, but keep everything consistent.'

    And crucially — you get a dial. At one end, 'trust this forecast completely, pin it exactly.' At the other end, 'it's just a hint, let the model mostly do its own thing.' And anywhere in between."

    26–31 min · Load the WEO and turn the dial¶

    What you do. Pull in the IMF's published forecast for the US and watch it enter the model.

    How. 1. On the left panel, open External forecasts. 2. Click Load WEO. (This loads the IMF's published growth and inflation numbers for the country you're viewing.) 3. Look at the Inflation and GDP Growth charts: small teal diamonds appear — those are the outside forecast's numbers.

    Say this:

    "Those diamonds are what the IMF says. Right now the tool is blending them with its own model — notice the blue scenario line sits partway between the model and the diamonds. That's the default: take the forecast seriously, but not as gospel."

    1. Now find the trust slider (it runs from "Ext forecast" on the left to "BVAR" on the right). Drag it all the way to "Ext forecast."

    What you find. - The blue scenario line snaps exactly onto the teal diamonds. (Verified: it lands on every diamond precisely.) You've said "trust the forecast completely." - Drag the slider toward "BVAR" and the line pulls away from the diamonds, back toward the model's own view. The caption under the slider tells you in words how much weight you're giving the forecast ("82% weight on the forecast," "pinned exactly," "model only").

    Say this (the intuition):

    "This is the honest way to use someone else's number. If you're sure, pin it. If you're only half-sure, say so — and the model spreads that half-sureness across the whole picture, so inflation and rates respond to the believable part of the forecast and no more."

    1. Point out the On / Off switch at the top of the panel — one click removes the outside forecast entirely and returns to your own scenario. And Export template hands you an Excel sheet you can fill with your own desk numbers and re-import the same way.

    What you find. The audience has seen a forecast go from "someone else's PDF" to "a live, adjustable ingredient in a consistent model." This is the feature that turns the tool into part of a real forecasting round.

    Part 5 — How one country moves the others (31–38 min)¶

    31–34 min · Why spillovers matter¶

    What you do. Set up the idea that economies are linked.

    Say this:

    "So far we've been in one country. But the US doesn't move alone — when US demand rises, it buys more from Europe, Japan, China; their exports rise; their central banks react. The tool knows these trade and financial links for fifty economies. Turning them on lets a shock travel."

    34–38 min · Solve the linked system¶

    What you do. Push the US, then let the effect ripple to its partners.

    How. 1. Make sure you still have a US scenario (your growth nudge, or the WEO pinned). 2. On the left, open Cross-border spillovers and click ▶ Solve full GVAR. 3. Wait a few seconds — a badge shows it's working. Then switch the Country selector to Euro Area (or Japan).

    Say this while it solves:

    "This is the one calculation that takes a moment, because the tool is solving all the economies together — the US pushes Europe, Europe pushes back, until everything is mutually consistent. That back-and-forth is exactly what a spreadsheet can't do."

    What you find. Europe's (or Japan's) forecast has shifted in response to what you did to the US — even though you never touched Europe directly. The shock travelled through trade and financial linkages, the way it does in the real world.

    Say this (tie it back):

    "And notice: if I pinned the US to the IMF's forecast and hit solve, the tool keeps the US exactly on those numbers and works out what they imply for everyone else. That's a full, consistent, multi-country scenario — from one afternoon's judgement."

    (Presenter note: there are two spillover switches. The one you used propagates a scenario across borders. The second, "Long-run target spillovers," lets a long-run anchor in one country nudge the others. Mention it exists; don't dwell.)

    Part 6 — Two power tools: policy counterfactuals & uncertainty (38–43 min)¶

    38–41 min · "What if the central bank did something different?"¶

    What you do. Show the policy-counterfactual mode briefly — it's the tool's most specialised feature.

    How. 1. On the left, open Policy counterfactual and switch it on (the panel turns orange). 2. You'll see an interest-rate adjustment with sliders for the next several quarters. Nudge the first one — say, cut the rate by 0.5 points for a few quarters.

    Say this:

    "Everything so far asked 'what if the economy does X?' This asks a sharper question: 'what if the central bank does X?' Here I hold rates lower than the rule would, and a separate, structural policy model works out the consequences for growth and inflation — a genuine policy experiment, not just a re-drawing of lines."

    What you find. An extra line appears showing the economy under the alternative policy, next to your baseline scenario. (You can also switch the policy objective — more weight on inflation vs. on employment — but that's a deeper dive; flag it and move on.)

    41–43 min · Put error bars on everything¶

    What you do. Turn on uncertainty so the audience sees ranges, not false precision.

    How. 1. In the top bar, set Uncertainty to Quick. 2. Click Compute. Wait a moment.

    Say this:

    "A single line pretends we know the future exactly. We don't. 'Compute' draws the fan of plausible outcomes around your scenario — wide where the model is unsure, narrow where you've pinned things down. Always show the fan; it's the honest picture."

    What you find. Shaded bands bloom around every scenario line. Where you pinned a value hard, the band pinches in; where you let the model run, it flares out. The uncertainty responds to your judgement.

    Part 7 — Keep your work, and where to learn more (43–45 min)¶

    43–45 min · Save, share, and close¶

    What you do. Show that a whole afternoon's work is one file, and point to the manual.

    How. - Export session (top of the left panel) saves everything — every country, every pin, every target, every imported forecast — into one file. Import session brings it all back, on any machine. This is how you hand a scenario to a colleague. - The scenario bar at the bottom also exports the charts and numbers as Excel, CSV, PNG, or PDF for a briefing. - Point to User Manual and Docs in the top bar for the full detail.

    Say this to close:

    "So, in forty-five minutes: we started from a data-driven forecast, sketched our own scenario by hand, anchored the long run, folded in an outside forecast at exactly the level of trust we chose, let it ripple across the world, ran a policy experiment, and put honest error bands on all of it — and we can hand the whole thing to a colleague as a single file. That's the tool. Now it's yours to play with."

    What you find. The audience leaves with a mental model (data-driven baseline → my what-ifs → consistent answer), the five moves that matter (drag, anchor, import, spillover, compute), and the confidence to open it themselves.

    Appendix · A 60-second version (if you're short on time)¶

    1. This is a scenario machine on top of a data-driven forecast of 50 economies.
    2. Right-click a chart to start conditioning, then drag the dot on GDP → the whole economy responds consistently.
    3. Long-run targets anchor where things settle.
    4. Load WEO + the trust slider → fold in an outside forecast, from "just a hint" to "pin it exactly."
    5. Solve full GVAR → the shock travels to other countries.
    6. Compute → honest uncertainty bands.
    7. Export session → hand the whole thing to a colleague.

    Appendix · Presenter's cheat-sheet of plain-language translations¶

    Use these words with a lay audience; avoid the ones in brackets.

    Say this Not this
    "the model's own forecast" the BVAR baseline / posterior mean
    "your scenario / your what-if" the conditional forecast
    "pin a value" / "nudge a path" impose a hard/soft conditioning constraint
    "how much you trust the forecast" the measurement-error variance / trust dial
    "where things settle in the long run" the steady-state / long-run anchor / drift
    "let the shock travel to other countries" propagate through the GVAR fixed point
    "the fan of plausible outcomes" the predictive density / posterior bands
    "a policy experiment" the DSGE counterfactual / IRF
